# What did you think "dark money" was before the webinar? What was correct about your definition, and what is different?
Before the webinar, I was under the impression that "dark money" was some sort of under-the-table transaction or a private exchange that could result in consequences if made public. I was correct in thinking that it involved money being hidden, and also that the people behind it might not want their identities to be known. However, I learned that dark money is more specifically connected to political spending and that it doesn't always involve a direct transaction to a certain candidate. Dark money can come through non-profits, trade associations, or LLCs that donate to super PACs, which makes it difficult to be able to identify the original donors. I also didn't realize how much money was involved, or how common these organizations had become in elections all over. The webinar helped me to understand that dark money is not just about a secret exchange, but rather about the difficulty of tracing political funding back and understanding who is attempting to influence elections. 

# Which limitation discussed in the webinar would most affect your confidence in a dark-money investigation? Explain why. How can reporters overcome that limitation or address it in their investigations?
The limitation that would most affect my confidence in a dark-money investigation would be not always being able to identify the original donor behind the money. I think it would be challenging to feel confident in an investigation if I could trace money to a nonprofit or a donor-advised fund, but couldn't confirm who originally provided it. The presenter explained how money can move through several organizations, creating a trail that is hard to follow accurately. He also made a point of saying that without subpoena power, reporters often can't go any further than that. This stood out to me because even if a reporter spends a really long time investigating, there could likely still be information that they cannot access. 
To address this limitation, I would look into as many public records as possible and be clear about what could actually be proven. The presenter mentioned using ProPublica's nonprofit explorer to look up an organization's name, EIN, and other identifying information across nonprofit filings. I would also analyze FEC records, corporate filings, and any available information about the organizer's officers or connections. Even if I am unable to identify the original donor, I could still report on the organizations that I do know are involved and explain where the money could be traced to, while making sure that I am not showcasing my assumptions as confirmed facts.

# Choose one reporting strategy from the webinar that you would priotize if you had limited time to investigate a political mailer, digital ad or mysterious nonprofit. Explain why you chose it and describe the first steps you would take. Incorporate one specific tip, example or warning from the presenter.
With limited time to investigate a political mailer, digital ad, or mysterious nonprofit, I would prioritize looking into the organization behind the ad through public records, starting specifically with the FEC database and ProPublica's Nonprofit Explorer. This strategy would give me a starting point and help me to figure out who is funding the message before spending too much time looking into other details. Firstly, I would identify who paid for the ad and search for that organization's name in the FEC database; from there, I would look for its EIN, other organizations it has given money to or received money from, and any connections to politial groups or individuals. 
One tip from the presenter that I would take into account is searching for both the full EIN and a shortened version of it in the nonprofit explorer. He explains how typos and differences in formatting can cause people to miss information, so searching in multiple ways can help find more records. I would also look at the organization's officers, agents, and business address for possible leads into more information. The presenter gave an example of Tom Foolery LLC, where the listed address was a skyscraper with no suite number, raising questions about the legitimacy of the company. I would use details such as these to identify potential connections and then decide where to focus the rest of the reporting.
