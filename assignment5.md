# Assignment 5 - writing about data
[Dollars to Megabits: You May Be Paying 400 Times As Much As Your Neighbor for Internet Service](https://themarkup.org/still-loading/2022/10/19/dollars-to-megabits-you-may-be-paying-400-times-as-much-as-your-neighbor-for-internet-service)
## Lede
I think the lede is effective because it starts with a real person experiencing the problem rather than immediately introducing statistics. Shirley Neville’s unreliable internet in New Orleans gives readers a concrete example of how slow service affects everyday life. The comparison to Lakeview, where residents receive internet almost 400 times faster for the same $55 monthly price, makes the disparity especially clear. It gives readers a reason to care before introducing the larger investigation.

## The data
The Markup analyzed more than 800,000 internet service offers from AT&T, Verizon, EarthLink and CenturyLink across 38 cities. The data showed that lower-income neighborhoods received the worst deals in nine out of 10 cities, while the least-White neighborhoods received the worst offers in two-thirds of the cities where there was enough data to compare. I think the scale of the data makes the findings convincing, especially because the reporters found similar patterns across multiple providers and cities.

## Sources
The reporters use a good balance of personal experiences, experts and company representatives. Shirley Neville, Pamela Jackson-Walters and other residents help show how poor internet affects people's daily lives. Experts like Chad Marlow and Christopher Lewis explain why internet access is a larger issue involving education, employment and participation in society. The article also includes responses from the internet providers, which gives readers a chance to understand their explanations. I especially appreciated that the reporters included AT&T's argument about household density and explained how they tested that claim.

## Story Structure
The story mostly follows an inverted-pyramid structure. It opens with Neville's experience, introduces the larger findings and then expands into examples from cities like Las Vegas, Detroit and Kansas City. It also uses elements of the martini-glass structure by starting with the broad investigation before moving into more detailed examples and individual stories. The article does not follow a strict timeline, but instead organizes the information by theme and location.

## Numbers
The numbers are one of the strongest parts of the story. The comparison between 300 Mbps and 5 Mbps for the same $55 monthly price is easy to understand, while the CenturyLink example of customers paying $0.25 versus $100 per Mbps makes the financial disparity even more striking. The reporters also explain the significance of 25 Mbps, the FCC's definition of broadband at the time. I think these comparisons make a complicated issue much easier for readers to understand.

## Data Visualization
The visuals help make the data more accessible. I especially like the opening graphic comparing two Kansas City households just 0.4 miles apart. It shows their internet speeds, monthly costs, household incomes and racial demographics side by side. The historical redlining map is also effective because it shows how past housing discrimination connects to present-day internet access. I think an interactive map where readers could enter their own address and compare available internet offers would make the story even more engaging.

## Methods
The article explains that the reporters collected offers using internet providers' online service lookup tools. It also links to a separate methodology article, which gives readers a way to learn more about how the investigation was conducted. One limitation is that the story examines advertised internet speeds rather than confirming the actual speeds customers receive. The reporters also focus on 38 major cities, so the findings do not necessarily represent rural communities. I appreciate that the article explains these boundaries instead of presenting the findings as universal.

## Etc. 
One of the most interesting parts of the story is its connection to historical redlining. The reporters found that formerly redlined areas received the worst internet deals in all 22 cities where historical maps were available. This adds important historical context and shows that the disparities are part of a larger pattern. Overall, I think the story is effective because it combines a large dataset with personal experiences, making an otherwise technical issue feel relevant to everyday readers.
